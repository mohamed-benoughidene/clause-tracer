# Keep the numbering stack, localise everything else

**Verdict, English: yes.** For English contracts, the current method is the best fit for the app's promises, once the owner's three fixes land. A line-based numbering stack with a continuity check copies the strongest single feature of the best published contract-structure system. One NUPunkt call for sentence starts suits an exact-quote check, because NUPunkt errs towards long sentences, and long sentences are safe. **Verdict, Arabic and French: not as built, but the design is right.** The skeleton (line walk, continuity check, tiling, paragraph fallback) is language-neutral and should stay. The vocabulary inside it and the sentence step are English-only today, and a probe shows Arabic and Japanese contracts fall to paragraphs with no warning. The route is per-language data tables around the same skeleton, plus a sentence splitter chosen per language. A neural splitter is a second opinion at most. Evidence is thin on three points: there is no independent benchmark of NUPunkt on contracts, no study of how wrong or missed breaks affect exact-quote checks, and little sourced material on Arabic and French contract conventions. This report keeps sourced facts and my judgement apart. Where a claim is my judgement, it says so.

## What the sources support for English

**Sourced facts.** The closest published design is Koreeda and Manning's. They use a "numbering transition" feature that tracks the largest number seen per numbering type and labels the next block as consecutive, a new level, or none ([Koreeda and Manning 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00150)). Their numbering baseline beat a layout-only PDF tool on their data, and the full system reached **0.914 structure accuracy on English NDA PDFs and 0.828 on plain-text NDAs**. Both sets are small, about 22 to 40 documents per type. Hegel et al. report about **0.90 F1 for clauses** and note that sub-clauses are "generally a harder problem", even for human annotators ([Hegel et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08128)). No paper compares a pure numbering splitter with an LLM splitter on contracts.

For sentences, NUPunkt is a Punkt-style model with a legal abbreviation list. It is pure Python, MIT-licensed, has no runtime dependencies, and is maintained (0.8.0, September 2026) ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/nupunkt/json)). Its paper reports **precision 0.911 but F1 0.725**, behind CharBoundary on F1. On the four English MultiLegalSBD sets its recall is only 0.43 to 0.44 ([NUPunkt paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04131)). Its own authors say 94% of its misses have no sentence-final punctuation: headings, list items, semicolons and colons ([accuracy notes](https://github.com/alea-institute/NUPunkt/blob/main/docs/benchmarks/accuracy.md)). The repo audit found the English abbreviation handling works: `cl. 5.2.`, `No. 5` and `Dr. Smith, Inc.` survived the probes (repo audit of `src/clause_tracer/ingest/`).

**My judgement.** The numbering stack is the right core. It is deterministic, offline, free, and every failure is visible at a number. NUPunkt's weakness (missed breaks) fits this app. The draw check is a substring test on the original text, so a longer sentence only makes the user read more. A wrong break is worse, because a quote that crosses the cut fails with "Check [clause] yourself" (the app's own check in `core/draw_check.py`). The authors' claim that false breaks hurt retrieval is an argument, not a measurement. Sentence tiling by construction means a bad NUPunkt answer can never lose text. That is a strong choice to keep.

**Where the English method is weak.** The audit found one limit that costs more than the owner's three. One skipped number (a deleted clause, "[intentionally omitted]") swallows every clause until the next sibling of an open ancestor. The owner accepted this for now and wants the ten test contracts to show how much it matters. Tables also become one sentence. No source gives failure rates for numbering parsers by type, so nobody can price this from the literature.

## How the owner's three fixes fit

The owner found three limits and has decided to fix them: a colon lead-in joined to the first definition, a long signature line left inside clause 14.6, and golden previews cut at 60 characters. The fixes are break after a colon line, a new clause for lines starting Signed, Executed or In witness whereof, and a full-text view flag. All three fit the recommendation. None adds a second sentence splitter or a model.

The colon fix has outside support. The MultiLegalSBD guidelines say a colon is not a sentence end "unless the colon is immediately followed by a newline", because that pattern introduces a list that should be annotated apart from its lead-in ([MultiLegalSBD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.01211)). Those rules come from French laws and judgments, not contracts. The trigger is a line ending in a colon, so it carries over to French and Arabic once trailing spaces and the fullwidth colon are handled (my judgement). NUPunkt also ships an opt-in `line_breaks=True` mode, but its docs say hard-wrapped text should keep it off ([NUPunkt comparison notes](https://github.com/alea-institute/NUPunkt/blob/main/docs/benchmarks/comparison.md)). A rule in the app's own code keeps the single call unchanged and the rule visible.

The signature fix is a keyword list, and no source covers signature blocks. It works, but it is the first English entry in a table that other languages will need their own version of. It also bypasses the real cause: the 60-character, eight-word heading limit and the `isupper()` test (`_markers.py:275-279`). I would write the list as data from the start. French and Arabic equivalents exist but no source I found confirms them ("Fait à ...", "الطرف الأول"), so they are unconfirmed.

The full-text flag does not change splitting. It matters more than it looks: a native reader can only sign off a French or Arabic golden file if the preview shows the whole sentence. Two cautions follow. The colon and signature fixes change clause and sentence ids, and NUPunkt is pinned precisely because "sentence ids depend on where it puts sentence breaks" (`docs/plans/stage-02-ingest.md:77`). The owner must therefore regenerate the goldens with the named command, and the expected values stay the owner's call (Constitution T11).

## Why the current method is not ready for Arabic and French

**Sourced facts, from the repo audit's probes.** French works only for `Article N - Title`. `Article 1er`, `Section 1.`, `Annexe` and `§ 1` fall to paragraphs. A non-breaking space after a marker, common in French and in PDF text, makes the whole text paragraphs. NUPunkt cut `l'art. 5.` after `art.`, so a French reference splits in two. Arabic `المادة 1:`, Arabic-Indic digits and the list letters `(أ)` give paragraph clauses with no hierarchy. NUPunkt did not break after the Arabic question mark `؟`. The Japanese `。` did not split either. The not-English warning is not built, so none of this is flagged.

NUPunkt itself "ships English models only; training your own per language is possible" ([comparison notes](https://github.com/alea-institute/NUPunkt/blob/main/docs/benchmarks/comparison.md)). Training is unsupervised from plain text. On French judgments, training NLTK Punkt on legal data raised F1 from **72.5 to 82.9**, which the authors credit to better abbreviation learning ([MultiLegalSBD](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.01211)). Off-the-shelf splitters were "subpar" on legal text in every language tested.

**Sourced facts on conventions.** Arabic list letters follow the abjad order (أ ب ج), not the dictionary order ([Wikipedia, Abjad numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjad_numerals)). Eastern Arabic-Indic digits sit at U+0660 to U+0669, while the Maghreb mostly uses Western digits ([Wikipedia, Eastern Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Arabic_numerals)). Arabic has no capital letters ([Wikipedia, Arabic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_alphabet)), so the heading test and the sentence rules that key on capitals do nothing there. Numbers are weak in the bidi algorithm, so a leading number never sets paragraph direction ([UAX 9](https://unicode.org/reports/tr9/)). Algerian contracts mix Arabic and French, and the Algerian text says French is "dominant in business and professional circles" ([Wikipedia, Languages of Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Algeria)). Evidence for per-language keyword lists and drafting styles is thin: the researcher's fetches of French and Spanish legal sources failed, so those lists are general knowledge, not sourced.

**My judgement.** The skeleton needs little change: it works on integers and logical text order. The vocabulary needs data tables: keywords, month-name guard, abbreviations, signature starters, heading limits per script. Unicode digit matching (`\p{Nd}`), non-breaking-space handling and bidi-control stripping are code changes. I rank the likely first failures as ASCII-only digits and letters, then capital-letter assumptions, then the full stop as the only terminator, then English keywords. That ranking is inferred, not measured. Arabic costs more than French: new digits, abjad letters, new terminators and no case. Bilingual layouts add a further problem that no source tested. Interleaved two-column PDF extraction would join right-to-left and left-to-right text into one sentence.

## Ranked options and when another wins

| Rank | Option | Why | Wins instead when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Code-owned clause skeleton, per-language data tables, a sentence step chosen per language (NUPunkt for English), a language gate, "unsure" fallback | Deterministic, tiny, offline, explainable failures. Matches the "never guess" rule. Effort is days per language with a native reviewer (my estimate) | Default for well-numbered contracts |
| 2 | Rank 1 plus a bundled neural splitter (SaT, wtpsplit) used only to flag "unsure" where it disagrees | Covers languages with no table. SaT is MIT and covers 85 languages ([SaT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16678)). It never moves a boundary | You must add many languages fast with no native reviewers |
| 3 | Table-driven sentence rules alone (pySBD style) | Simple. But pySBD scored 37.4 F1 on Arabic against 80.7 for SaT with its small-model variant ([SaT paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16678)), and it has had no release since 2021 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/pysbd/json)) | Rarely. A fixed English-only test |
| 4 | Neural splitter as the main splitter | Best breadth, but probabilistic, 400 to 560 MB of ONNX model, silent false splits. Determinism across machines is unconfirmed | Chinese, Japanese or Thai, where tables fail (my judgement) |
| 5 | AI fallback for splitting | LLMs altered 1.5 to 2% of characters even when told not to ([SaT paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16678)). Breaks "same answer every run" | Only on a user-chosen case, with the output checked as spans of the original |

The ranking is my judgement. The only source that ranks these options for a similar product is the research note itself, and it is also judgement. For the sentence step per language, I would test three things in order: French abbreviations added to the existing rules, a NUPunkt model trained on French legal text, and the SaT second opinion. Swapping the sentence splitter changes sentence ids, so the splitter name and version must be recorded with the ids and stored reviews migrated (repo audit, `docs/system-design.md:352`).

## Next steps

1. Land the three fixes, regenerate the goldens with the named command, and add a table case for each (colon, signature, full-text flag). Tell the owner the ids moved.
2. Build the not-English warning and make the paragraph fallback say so. Today a French or Arabic contract degrades without a signal.
3. Hand-label clause starts and nesting on the ten English test contracts. Measure clause-start precision and recall, and the wrong-break rate per 1,000 boundaries. Use the result to decide the skipped-number rule. The note on testing proposes zero wrong breaks on goldens as a gate, which is a suggestion for the owner, not a standard.
4. Propose an ADR, marked proposed, that moves marker words, abbreviations, signature starters, month guards and heading limits into per-language tables, and adds `\p{Nd}`, non-breaking-space and bidi handling. The owner decides before anyone builds.
5. Pilot French first. Seed tests from the MultiLegalSBD French sets (judgments and laws, not contracts) and add 30 to 100 own contract snippets reviewed by a native reader.
6. For Arabic, write the goldens yourself with a native reviewer. No public Arabic contract boundary set was found. Add `؟` and `؛`, abjad letters and Arabic-Indic digits.
7. Only after steps 1 to 6, trial SaT as a second opinion, and check size, speed and determinism in a real build.

## Conclusion

The owner's three fixes are not patches on a doomed design. They are the first language-neutral rules (a line ending in a colon) and the first entries of a table (signature starters). That is the same shape the multilingual design needs, so the fixes move the app towards it. The sources also change one assumption: the sentence splitter is the part to replace per language, not the clause skeleton. NUPunkt's unsupervised design makes a French model a plausible and cheap trial, though no source has run it.

The weak point is measurement. Every accuracy number for NUPunkt comes from court opinions, laws or its authors' own sets. One thing needs a check: ADR 0007 quotes an F1 of 0.782 for NUPunkt, but the paper gives 0.725 for NUPunkt and 0.782 for CharBoundary. Ten hand-labelled English contracts and thirty reviewed snippets per new language will tell the owner more about this app than any benchmark found here.
